# DEEP AUDIT 1 — LEGACY ESBE HYDRONIC ACTUATOR / SHUNT-CONTROL SUPERSESSION

## 1. EXECUTIVE THESIS

Sweden contains a long tail of ESBE hydronic valves and actuators installed across boilers, hydronic heating systems and larger building plant. ESBE itself provides unusually authoritative supersession and compatibility data, while Swedish retailers carry the new actuators and adapter kits. The potentially ownable object is **not “sell ESBE actuators”**; it is a deterministic **old actuator / old valve / old control signal → current actuator + required adapter/valve migration + locally stocked offer** graph. The candidate remains unproven because Sweden-specific installed-base size, consumer-versus-trade demand and wholesale economics are unresolved.

## 2. WHY THIS EXISTS

ESBE was founded in Sweden in 1906. The company says its shunt became standard equipment on many oil boilers across Europe, that the M60 motor introduced in 1992 fit nearly all smaller heating systems, and that its hydronic controls now regulate comfort in **millions of homes across Europe**. Series 80 actuators date back to the late 1970s; Series 90 was introduced in 1999. That creates a genuine multi-decade legacy tail, although a defensible Sweden-specific surviving count remains UNKNOWN.

Lifecycle trigger is FAILURE / OBSOLESCENCE / COMPATIBILITY_UPGRADE. An old actuator or controller fails; the owner/technician needs to know whether the current successor is electrically and mechanically equivalent, or whether the valve/adapter must also change.

## 19. HYPOTHESIS LEDGER

**H1:** There is a meaningful Swedish tail of legacy ESBE actuator/valve installations whose replacement decision is technically documented by ESBE but commercially fragmented across current-SKU VVS stores, creating an ownable compatibility-commerce graph.

**H0:** The meaningful market is predominantly professional, ESBE's own replacement guidance plus existing VVS merchants already resolves the decision well enough, and dealer margins/support burden make a new specialist layer unnecessary.

**Strongest evidence for H1:**
- multi-decade Series 80 → Series 90 lineage;
- explicit OEM supersession chains;
- real negative/conditional rules such as M60 not fitting VRG and adapter requirements;
- current Swedish inventory and checkout for the successor products;
- old references are not obviously the primary retail navigation surface.

**Strongest evidence against H1:**
- ESBE itself has unusually strong support and selection guidance;
- Swedish VVS ecommerce is mature and exposes RSK/MPN/GTIN cleanly;
- electricians/HVAC technicians may already be the primary decision makers;
- no Sweden-specific installed-base count was found.

**Falsifier:** Any of the following should kill the candidate:
- exact old-reference demand is negligible;
- a Swedish specialist is found that already provides old-label → current part + adapter + checkout comprehensively;
- wholesale economics do not support contribution after support/returns;
- reseller access is unavailable;
- most sales require installer-only diagnosis rather than deterministic self-service identification.

**Decisive unknown:** reseller economics and exact old-reference commercial demand.

**Highest-EVI next test:** supplier/distributor contact for dealer terms and product-feed access, immediately followed by authenticated Swedish exact-query demand measurement if economics survive.

## MOST IMPORTANT KILL

**Swedish residential ventilation spares, especially Östberg HERU / Bahco-Fläkt / Systemair-Villavent.**

Building another specialist store would be competition, not ownership.

## NEW REUSABLE MARKET MECHANISM

**Legacy-identifier compiler:**

`old OEM label / superseded part number`
→ `electrical/control/interface constraints`
→ `current successor`
→ `required adapter or paired replacement`
→ `negative compatibility`
→ `local RSK/MPN/GTIN offer`
→ `checkout`

The best future niches may be found by discovering **old identifiers with authoritative supersession tables but no local transaction compiler**, rather than merely looking for “bad spare-parts websites.”
